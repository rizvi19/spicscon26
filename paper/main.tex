\begin{abstract}
Indoor positioning remains difficult when low-cost sensing modalities fail in different ways. WiFi received-signal-strength-indicator (RSSI) measurements are widely available but fluctuate under multipath, interference, and body shadowing, while visible-light-positioning (VLP) cues can be spatially sharp but are sensitive to blockage and illumination changes. This paper presents a simulation-based robustness study of hybrid indoor positioning using WiFi RSSI-like and visible-light intensity-like measurements. The evaluated framework examines lightweight DSP filtering, converts modality evidence into probability maps over a $10\times10$ room grid, smooths spatial priors using graph diffusion, and selects among predefined fusion modes using a non-oracle reliability-aware adaptive policy. Experiments use a $10\,\mathrm{m}\times10\,\mathrm{m}$ room, 100 candidate cells, three WiFi access points, four LED anchors, synthetic walking trajectories, and five repeated test seeds under clean, WiFi-degraded, VLP-blocked, and mixed-dynamic scenarios. Adaptive fusion is not universally best: static fusion gives the lowest clean-case mean error, and VLP-only estimation is best when only WiFi is degraded. Under optical blockage and mixed degradation, however, adaptive fusion reduces mean error by approximately 25.86\% and 19.50\%, respectively, relative to static fusion. A small smartphone-based physical sanity check is used only to motivate distance- and blockage-sensitive simulated degradation trends. The study provides a reproducible stress test for reliability-aware hybrid fusion rather than a deployment claim.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Indoor positioning supports smart buildings, robotics, assistive navigation, asset tracking, context-aware services, and emergency response. Reliable indoor localization remains difficult because satellite-based positioning is weak indoors, while low-cost signals are often unstable. WiFi RSSI is attractive because infrastructure is already deployed, but RSSI varies with multipath, device orientation, interference, human blockage, and environmental changes. Visible-light positioning (VLP) provides a complementary optical cue because LEDs can act as spatial anchors, but optical evidence is vulnerable to line-of-sight blockage, receiver orientation, dimming, and illumination variation.

This creates a practical fusion problem. A fixed fusion rule can work when both modalities are stable, but the same fixed rule can be harmful when one modality becomes unreliable. If WiFi is noisy while visible-light evidence remains clean, an optical-dominant estimate may be preferable. If VLP is blocked, the estimator should reduce optical trust and rely more on radio and spatial continuity. Therefore, the key challenge studied here is how a low-cost hybrid estimator should behave when the reliability of the two modalities changes over time.

Classic WiFi systems established RF fingerprinting and probabilistic location estimation \cite{bahl2000radar,youssef2005horus}; later work continued to examine robust wireless localization and WiFi-assisted positioning principles \cite{ayyalasomayajula2020dloc,dai2023wifi}. VLP surveys identify optical blockage, receiver assumptions, and implementation constraints as central practical issues \cite{do2016vlc,luo2017vlpstate,afzalan2019vlp}. Hybrid RF--optical systems show that complementary modalities can improve localization, but many strong systems depend on specific hardware, datasets, or static fusion assumptions. This leaves a narrower but useful gap: a reproducible, low-resource stress test that isolates how simple DSP filtering, topology-aware graph smoothing, and reliability-aware fusion behave under controlled WiFi noise and visible-light blockage.

This paper addresses that gap using simulation-based evidence rather than a real deployment. The work uses physically motivated RSSI-like and intensity-like signal models, repeated seeds, controlled degradation scenarios, and ablation comparisons. The final adaptive policy is intentionally transparent. It selects among predefined WiFi-heavy, VLP-heavy, balanced, graph-prior-heavy, and graph-recovery modes using only observable reliability cues, such as probability-map confidence, entropy, recent signal variance, and intensity-drop indicators. It does not use the true position, test-time scenario label, or ground-truth degradation flag for fusion. Early planning was motivated by adaptive and reinforcement-learning-style fusion ideas \cite{salimibeni2022rlfusion}, but the implemented method favors traceability and reproducibility rather than claiming a new RL algorithm.

The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a reproducible simulation framework for hybrid WiFi RSSI-like and visible-light intensity-like indoor positioning under clean, WiFi-degraded, VLP-blocked, and mixed-dynamic scenarios;
    \item a compact DSP--graph-signal-processing pipeline that converts signal streams into grid-cell probability maps and smooths spatial priors over a room graph;
    \item a lightweight, non-oracle reliability-aware adaptive fusion policy evaluated against WiFi-only, VLP-only, static fusion, DSP fusion, and graph-smoothed fusion baselines across repeated test seeds.
\end{itemize}
The main result is deliberately conditional. Adaptive fusion is not always best. Static fusion is strongest in the clean scenario, and VLP-only estimation is strongest when only WiFi is degraded. Adaptive fusion is most helpful under simulated optical blockage and mixed degradation, where signal reliability changes over time.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{WiFi and RSSI-Based Indoor Positioning}
WiFi positioning has been studied for more than two decades, from RSSI fingerprinting to probabilistic and learning-based localization. RADAR showed the feasibility of RF-based indoor user tracking using existing wireless infrastructure \cite{bahl2000radar}. Horus introduced probabilistic WLAN location determination and demonstrated the value of statistical modeling for indoor RSSI uncertainty \cite{youssef2005horus}. More recent work has used deep learning and wireless signal modeling for indoor navigation \cite{ayyalasomayajula2020dloc}. Surveys also show that WiFi-assisted positioning remains active because the infrastructure is common and inexpensive \cite{dai2023wifi}. However, WiFi RSSI remains sensitive to multipath, attenuation, and environmental variability. Our work does not use channel state information or measured fingerprint databases. Instead, it uses controlled RSSI-like degradation to test fusion behavior.

\subsection{Visible-Light Positioning}
VLP uses illumination infrastructure as a positioning source. Surveys describe RSS-based, angle-based, image-sensor-based, and hybrid VLP approaches, along with the effects of receiver geometry, line-of-sight availability, and hardware assumptions \cite{do2016vlc,luo2017vlpstate,afzalan2019vlp}. Recent optical tracking work using extended Kalman filtering illustrates how VLP can be used for position and orientation estimation when the optical channel is available \cite{saengudomlert2025aeu_ekf_vlp}. However, practical VLP performance can degrade under blockage, dimming, receiver orientation change, and ambient light variation. In this paper, VLP is represented by a simplified intensity-like model for controlled simulation, not by a calibrated photodiode or camera receiver.

\subsection{Hybrid Fusion, Graph Processing, and Adaptive Policies}
Hybrid RF--optical localization is attractive because radio and optical channels fail differently. Recent RF--VLP and LiFi--WiFi works motivate the use of complementary modalities for indoor localization \cite{lu2025rfvlp,mustafa2025lifiwifi}. The present study does not claim direct performance comparison to those hardware or learning-supported systems. Instead, it focuses on reproducible stress testing of fusion choices under controlled failures. This distinction is important: external VLP, RF--VLP, EKF, and deep-learning systems use different sensors, calibration assumptions, room layouts, or datasets, whereas the fair comparison here is among methods receiving the same synthetic observations.

Graph signal processing (GSP) provides tools for analyzing signals defined on irregular domains \cite{shuman2013gsp,ortega2018gsp}. An indoor grid can be represented as a graph where adjacent cells encode local spatial continuity. Related graph-based localization work uses graph structure for radio-fingerprint augmentation \cite{chen2023graphfingerprint}. In this paper, the graph component is narrower: a topology-aware smoothing layer over grid-cell probability maps.

Adaptive information fusion and RL-inspired localization have also been explored \cite{salimibeni2022rlfusion}. The contribution here is not a new learning algorithm. The paper instead evaluates a transparent reliability-aware fusion policy and asks a specific question: when WiFi and visible-light reliability change, does mode selection outperform fixed fusion in a controlled simulated environment?

\section{System Model and Problem Setup}
\subsection{Room, Anchors, and Trajectory}
The simulated room is $10\,\mathrm{m}\times10\,\mathrm{m}$ and is divided into a $10\times10$ grid, producing 100 candidate cells. Three WiFi access points and four LED anchors are placed at known coordinates. A 100-step synthetic walking trajectory is generated for each random seed. Fig.~\ref{fig:layout} shows the simulated room, anchors, grid, and a sample trajectory.

\subsection{WiFi RSSI-Like Signal Model}
For a candidate position $\mathbf{x}$ and access point $i$, the expected RSSI-like value is modeled as
\begin{equation}
 r_i(\mathbf{x}) = P_0 - 10n\log_{10}\!\left(\max(d_i(\mathbf{x}),d_{\min})\right),
 \label{eq:wifi}
\end{equation}
where $d_i(\mathbf{x})$ is the horizontal distance from $\mathbf{x}$ to access point $i$, $P_0=-40$ dBm is the reference RSSI level, $n=2.2$ is the path-loss exponent, and $d_{\min}$ avoids a singularity near an anchor. Gaussian noise, shadowing, and multipath-like bursts are added according to the scenario. This model is used only to generate controlled RSSI-like evidence; it is not proposed as a new WiFi propagation model.

\subsection{Visible-Light Intensity-Like Signal Model}
For LED $j$, the expected visible-light intensity-like value is
\begin{equation}
 \ell_j(\mathbf{x})=\frac{P_{\mathrm{LED}}h^2}{\left(d_{j,h}^{2}(\mathbf{x})+h^2\right)^2},
 \label{eq:vlp}
\end{equation}
where $d_{j,h}(\mathbf{x})$ is horizontal distance from the receiver to LED $j$, $P_{\mathrm{LED}}=1$ is a power scale, and $h=2$ m is the vertical separation between the assumed receiver plane at 1 m and the LED plane at 3 m. Optical noise and blockage attenuation are added according to the scenario. This is a simplified distance-sensitive optical model for simulation and does not model a full Lambertian channel, photodiode response, camera exposure, reflection, or ambient light dynamics.

\subsection{Physical Trend Check and Scenario Parameters}
A small smartphone-based trend check was used to support the direction of the simulated degradation assumptions, not to validate the full localization pipeline. The WiFi set contains 200 RSSI rows from 1--10 m under normal and blocked conditions. Mean normal RSSI changes from approximately $-29.1$ dBm at 1 m to $-49.1$ dBm at 10 m, and the average blocked-minus-normal difference is $-14.95$ dB. The light set contains 200 app-estimated rows from 1--10 m under unblocked and blocked conditions; mean unblocked intensity changes from approximately 627.7 at 1 m to 39.1 at 10 m, and the average blocked/unblocked ratio is 0.0289. These measurements motivate distance- and blockage-sensitive simulation settings, but the phone light-meter values are app estimates, not calibrated photodiode measurements.

\subsection{DSP Filtering and Probability Maps}
The RSSI-like and intensity-like streams are filtered using moving average, median, and exponential moving average filters. The filters are standard and lightweight. Their role is not to create a new DSP algorithm, but to provide reproducible denoising baselines. In the final ablation, median filtering is used as the representative DSP baseline because it suppresses burst-like outliers without requiring a learned model.

For each time step, Gaussian likelihoods compare the measured anchor vector with the expected anchor vector at every candidate grid cell. The likelihoods are normalized into WiFi and VLP probability maps, denoted by $\mathbf{p}_{w}$ and $\mathbf{p}_{v}$. A position estimate is obtained from the cell with the maximum probability or from the fused probability distribution, depending on the evaluated method. Because probability maps from different sensing modalities can have different sharpness, equal-weight static fusion is treated as a simple naive baseline rather than a calibrated optimum.

\subsection{Graph Smoothing}
The room grid is represented as a graph $G=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})$, where each candidate cell is a node and 4-neighbor adjacency defines edges. Given a static fused map $\mathbf{p}_{s}$, graph diffusion forms a topology-aware prior:
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf{p}_{g}=\mathcal{N}\!\left((1-\alpha)\mathbf{p}_{s}+\alpha\mathbf{A}_{n}\mathbf{p}_{s}\right),
 \label{eq:gsp}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{A}_{n}$ is the row-normalized adjacency matrix, $\alpha=0.25$, and $\mathcal{N}(\cdot)$ normalizes the probability vector. The graph step encourages local spatial consistency, reducing isolated probability spikes and unrealistic jumps. It is used as a smoothing layer over probability maps, not as a new graph-localization theory.

\subsection{Non-Oracle Reliability-Aware Adaptive Fusion}
For modality $m$, reliability is computed from peak probability, normalized entropy, and an observable signal-quality penalty:
\begin{equation}
 q_m=\max(\mathbf{p}_{m})\left(1-\frac{H(\mathbf{p}_{m})}{\log 100}\right)\gamma_m,
 \label{eq:reliability}
\end{equation}
where $H(\cdot)$ is entropy. The term $\gamma_m\in[0.25,1]$ is estimated only from measurements available at test time. For WiFi, it is reduced when recent RSSI variance and burst-like changes increase. For VLP, it is reduced when the current total light intensity drops sharply relative to a rolling local history. The true position, scenario name, and ground-truth blockage/noise labels are not used by the fusion policy.

The final fused probability map is
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf{p}_{f}=\mathcal{N}\!\left(w_w\mathbf{p}_{w}+w_v\mathbf{p}_{v}+w_g\mathbf{p}_{g}\right).
 \label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
The weights $(w_w,w_v,w_g)$ are fixed inside each mode, and the adaptive element is interpretable switching among modes. The five mode weights are WiFi-heavy $(0.70,0.20,0.10)$, VLP-heavy $(0.20,0.70,0.10)$, balanced $(0.45,0.45,0.10)$, graph-prior-heavy $(0.30,0.30,0.40)$, and graph-recovery $(0.15,0.15,0.70)$. These weights are deliberately coarse design points rather than per-scenario optimized values: the first two emphasize one dominant modality, the balanced mode keeps equal radio--optical trust, and the two graph modes preserve a stronger spatial prior when both probability maps are uncertain. The same weights are frozen for every final test scenario.

\section{Experimental Setup}
The evaluated methods are WiFi-only, VLP-only, equal-weight static fusion, static fusion after median DSP filtering, graph-smoothed fusion, and the complete adaptive DSP+GSP fusion policy. The same room, anchors, trajectory-generation procedure, and scenario definitions are used across all methods. Final results use five repeated test seeds, 101--105. All parameters listed in Table~\ref{tab:setup} are kept fixed during the final test run.

Figures~\ref{fig:dsp} and \ref{fig:gsp} illustrate the two preprocessing components. DSP filters suppress short fluctuations but may lag or flatten useful clean measurements. Graph smoothing redistributes isolated probability peaks toward neighboring cells, which can stabilize a noisy spatial prior but may blur a confident clean map. These tradeoffs are why the proposed system does not apply all components blindly; the adaptive policy changes emphasis according to reliability.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\subsection{Scenario-Wise Localization Accuracy}
Table~\ref{tab:main} and Fig.~\ref{fig:error} show the main repeated-seed results. The first observation is that adaptive fusion is not universally best. In the clean scenario, static fusion gives the lowest mean error, $0.450\pm0.012$ m, with P90 error $0.754\pm0.032$ m. This is expected because both modalities are reliable, so a fixed rule does not need to avoid a damaged signal.

In the WiFi-degraded scenario, VLP-only estimation gives the best mean error, $0.451\pm0.018$ m. The reason is also intuitive: the optical modality remains clean, while WiFi is noisy. Adding a degraded radio modality is not beneficial when the clean optical evidence is already strong.

The benefit of adaptive fusion appears when the better modality changes. Under VLP blockage, adaptive fusion reaches mean error $0.751\pm0.031$ m, compared with $1.013\pm0.092$ m for static fusion. Under mixed degradation, adaptive fusion reaches $0.611\pm0.078$ m, compared with $0.759\pm0.101$ m for static fusion. Relative to the rounded Table~\ref{tab:main} values, adaptive fusion improves mean error by 25.86\% and P90 error by 40.83\% in the VLP-blocked scenario. In the mixed-dynamic scenario, the corresponding improvements are 19.50\% and 31.50\%.

\subsection{Ablation, Failure Rate, and Smoothness}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} isolates preprocessing effects in the two difficult scenarios. Median DSP reduces P90 error and failure rate under VLP blockage, and it gives the smoothest mixed-dynamic trajectories. Graph diffusion also reduces failure rate under VLP blockage. However, neither component alone dominates every metric. For example, unconditional graph diffusion increases mixed-dynamic mean error. This confirms that topology-aware smoothing is useful, but it must be applied carefully.

Fig.~\ref{fig:robust} shows the robustness summary. Against static fusion, adaptive fusion lowers failure rate by 0.094 under VLP blockage and by 0.060 under mixed degradation. From the rounded Table~\ref{tab:ablation} values, smoothness improves by 47.62\% and 41.66\%, respectively. These results support a conditional conclusion: adaptive weighting is useful when reliability changes, whereas clean or single-degradation settings may favor simpler estimators.

\subsection{Interpretation and Reproducibility Notes}
The experiments answer three practical research questions. First, simple DSP filtering is useful mainly as a stabilizing component: it reduces short-term fluctuations, but it should not be treated as universally beneficial because smoothing can also delay or flatten informative clean signals. Second, graph smoothing is useful as a spatial regularizer because indoor positions are locally connected; however, the ablation shows that applying a graph prior unconditionally can increase error when the underlying probability map is already reliable. Third, reliability-aware fusion is most valuable when the dominant modality changes over time. This explains why adaptive fusion does not improve the clean case, matches VLP-dominant behavior under WiFi degradation, and performs best under VLP blockage and mixed degradation.

The static-fusion and VLP-only rows are close in several cases because the VLP likelihood maps are sharper than the RSSI likelihood maps after independent probability normalization. This is reported intentionally: it shows that naive equal-weight probability fusion can be dominated by one modality and should not be interpreted as a calibrated optimal fusion rule. The adaptive policy partly addresses this by using confidence and entropy rather than fixed equal weights, but the result should still be read as a controlled robustness study rather than a universal localization benchmark.

The computational structure is lightweight. For $N=100$ cells and $E$ graph edges, each time step requires likelihood evaluation over the candidate cells and one sparse graph-diffusion operation of order $O(N+E)$. No neural network inference, camera processing, CSI extraction, particle filtering, or camera/photodiode reconstruction is used. Therefore, the framework is suitable as a low-resource simulation testbed, although this paper does not claim deployment-level runtime validation.

\section{Scope, Limitations, and Future Work}
This study is intentionally designed as a controlled, simulation-based robustness analysis rather than a full deployment study. The RSSI-like and VLP intensity-like models are simplified so that the effects of WiFi degradation, optical blockage, graph smoothing, and adaptive fusion can be isolated under repeatable conditions. This controlled setup supports reproducible comparison across baselines, but it does not replace evaluation with synchronized real WiFi and calibrated optical measurements.

The work also focuses on low-cost sensing assumptions. It does not use WiFi channel state information, camera-based VLP, photodiode calibration, or a measured fingerprint database. The small smartphone-based physical trend check partially supports the assumed distance- and blockage-sensitive trends, but it is not deployment-level validation of the complete localization pipeline. Because no common real dataset, hardware setup, or sensing stack is shared with the cited RF--VLP, LiFi--WiFi, or EKF-based VLP systems, this paper does not claim state-of-the-art superiority over external localization systems. Future work should evaluate the same design with real indoor measurements, richer optical channel models, device-orientation changes, multiple layouts, larger seed sets, calibrated modality-likelihood scaling, and external tracking baselines such as EKF or particle-filter fusion when comparable measurements are available.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presented a reproducible simulation-based robustness study of hybrid WiFi RSSI-like and visible-light intensity-like indoor positioning. The results show that no single method is best in all conditions. Static fusion is strongest when both modalities are clean, VLP-only estimation is strongest when only WiFi is degraded, and reliability-aware adaptive fusion is most useful under optical blockage and mixed degradation. Relative to static fusion, the adaptive policy reduces mean error by approximately 25.86\% for VLP blockage and 19.50\% for mixed degradation while also reducing failures and improving trajectory smoothness.

The broader value of the work is methodological. It provides a low-cost and reproducible way to stress-test hybrid indoor localization before expensive hardware collection. Future work should validate the framework using synchronized real WiFi measurements and calibrated optical sensing, then examine whether the transparent reliability policy remains effective in deployed indoor environments.
